Notes on Kidger's NDEs talks

This is a compilation of (not totally complete) notes on Patrick Kidger's talks across two to three seminars. The aim here is to provide a very high-level overview of subfields within NDEs.

NDEs Overview

A neural differential equation is a differential equation (DE) that uses a neural network (NN) with learnable parameters/functions to parametrize its vector field. A canonical starter example is a neural ordinary differential equation (NODE):

y(0)=y0dydt(t)=fθ(t,y(t))y(0) = y_0 \hspace{1cm} \frac{dy}{dt}(t) = f_\theta(t, y(t))y(0)=y0​dtdy​(t)=fθ​(t,y(t))

where fθ:R×Rd→Rdf_\theta: \mathbb{R} \times\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^dfθ​:R×Rd→Rd is some standard neural architecture, oftentimes a feed-forward one. The central idea here is that, instead of treating an ODE solver (e.g. RK4) as an external black-box that just produces numerical solutions, we now embed it inside an NN's differentiable computation graph.

Remark: Traditionally, solvers only integrate and simulate the evolution of DEs with fixed parameters (e.g. of a physical/biological system), without caring how their outputs vary with respect to those parameters. They are used to generate data for an NN, which is trained afterward on these simulated data to approximate the underlying system, making it a separate pipeline from the solver.

More ground-level details about NODE

In an NODE, the dynamics of the ODE are defined by an NN fθf_\thetafθ​ with learnable parameters. To train this NN, it's of course essential to understand how changes in θ\thetaθ influence the integrated solution yyy. By recording and encompassing the solver's internal numerical steps in the differentiable computation graph, auto-differentiation can propagate gradients from the loss function back through the solver and into the NN that defines how yyy evolves, "nudging" the parameters along the way so that the learned dynamics better match the data. This turns the solver into a trainable component of the model, enabling end-to-end learning where the system learns not just static (plain "input →\to→ output") mappings, but the continuous-time evolution described by the ODE itself.

Note that the ODE describes the evolution of yyy, which lives in a latent space (some internal representation of the raw input space). The solver integrates this NODE from y(0)y(0)y(0) (the encoded input) to y(T)y(T)y(T), then passes y(T)y(T)y(T) through affine and softmax layers to get output/predictions and compute a loss.

It's worth mentioning that this seemingly complicated nesting of an NN inside an DE is nothing groundbreaking from a mathematics or ML point of view alone. For example, the SIR model in epidemiology is as follows:

ddt(s(t)i(t)r(t))=(−bs(t)i(t)bs(t)i(t)−ki(t)ki(t)).\frac{d}{dt} \begin{pmatrix} s(t) \\ i(t) \\ r(t) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -b s(t)i(t) \\ b s(t)i(t) - k i(t) \\ k i(t) \end{pmatrix}.dtd​​s(t)i(t)r(t)​​=​−bs(t)i(t)bs(t)i(t)−ki(t)ki(t)​​.

Both NODEs and the SIR model are parametrized DEs with some theoretical models for the RHS, with the mere difference being that SIR is specialized to fit a specific phenomenon (namely the evolution of a disease), while an NODE is a generalized "fitter" applicable to lots of things thanks to the NN architecture. In fact, the SIR model can transform into an NODE by making the parameters bbb and kkk learnable.

In the framework of deep learning (DL), we may also see a shadow of NODE: by applying Euler method on dydt(t)=fθ(t,y(t))\frac{dy}{dt}(t) = f_\theta(t, y(t))dtdy​(t)=fθ​(t,y(t)), we obtain a residual network (ResNet):

ytn+1=ytn+Δtfθ(tn,ytn).y_{t_{n+1}} = y_{t_{n}} + \Delta tf_\theta(t_n, y_{t_{n}}).ytn+1​​=ytn​​+Δtfθ​(tn​,ytn​​).

Interestingly, this connection between DL architectures and NDEs is ubiquitous:

  1. ResNet ↔\leftrightarrow↔ neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs)
  2. GRUs/LSTMs ↔\leftrightarrow↔ neural controlled differential equations (NCDEs)
  3. StyleGAN2 ↔\leftrightarrow↔ neural stochastic differential equations (NSDEs)

NDEs have a myriad applications in real life, often demonstrating state-of-the-art performance in areas like systematic modelling (physical, biological, financial, etc), regular/irregular time series analysis, generative modelling, and many more.

NDEs offer practically a best-of-both-world approach: the NN side offers high-capacity function approximation and high trainability, while the DE side offers strong prior on model space, memory-efficiency (adjoint backpropagation), and theoretical understanding via well-understood mathematics.

However, NDEs are not without their limitations (speed of computation (might be an implementation issue regarding Python vs C++), number of parameters that insinuates low interpretability and potential overfitting), which we won't discuss much about here.

NCDEs Overview

What is a CDE?

Motivation: consider the integral form of the NODE we saw above:

y(0)=y0y(t)=y(0)+∫0tfθ(y(s))dsy(0) = y_0 \hspace{1cm} y(t) = y(0) + \int_0^t f_\theta(y(s))dsy(0)=y0​y(t)=y(0)+∫0t​fθ​(y(s))ds

which takes the initial condition as the single input. Here, an extra "artificial" time-like dimension is introduced and integrated over, so it's natural to ask if the model can be extended to data of sequential nature (e.g. time series). That is, if we observe some ordered data (y0,…,yn)(y_0,\ldots,y_n)(y0​,…,yn​) and would like to align this new dimension with the natural ordering of the data, how do we extend the y(0)=y0y(0)=y_0y(0)=y0​ condition to one resembling (with the timestamps not necessarily looking like) ‘‘y(0)=y0,…,y(n)=yn"``y(0)=y_0,\ldots,y(n)=y_n"‘‘y(0)=y0​,…,y(n)=yn​"? Since the initial condition at y(0)y(0)y(0) determines the solution of an ODE, there is no direct mechanism for incorporating the late-coming data. This is where CDEs come into play.

The main idea of a CDE is to have the local dynamics yyy of the system depend on some time-varying input (path) X:[0,T]→RvX:[0, T] \to \mathbb{R}^vX:[0,T]→Rv in the form of a Riemann-Stieltjes integral

y(t)=y(0)+∫0tf(y(s))dX(s)for t∈[0,T]y(t) = y(0) + \int_0^t f(y(s))dX(s) \hspace{0.5cm} \text{for } t\in [0,T]y(t)=y(0)+∫0t​f(y(s))dX(s)for t∈[0,T]

where f(y(s))dX(s)f(y(s))dX(s)f(y(s))dX(s) is a matrix-vector product and can be interpreted as f(y(s))dXds(s)dsf(y(s))\frac{dX}{ds}(s)dsf(y(s))dsdX​(s)ds as long as XXX is suitably smooth. XXX can be thought to determine some local notion of physics and fundamentally, a CDE is an operator on path space X↦yX \mapsto yX↦y that preserves measurability with respect to a filtration (i.e., causality)

What is an NCDE?

Let X:[0,T]→RvX:[0, T] \to \mathbb{R}^vX:[0,T]→Rv be some observed data, for example an interpolation of a time series, and our goal now is to learn NNs ζθ\zeta_\thetaζθ​, fθf_\thetafθ​, lθl_\thetalθ​ such that

y(0)=ζθ(X(0))y(t)=y(0)+∫0tfθ(y(s))dX(s)y(0) = \zeta_\theta(X(0)) \hspace{1cm} y(t) = y(0) + \int_0^tf_\theta(y(s))dX(s)y(0)=ζθ​(X(0))y(t)=y(0)+∫0t​fθ​(y(s))dX(s)

and the output is either lθ(y(T))l_\theta(y(T))lθ​(y(T)) or lθ(y(t))l_\theta(y(t))lθ​(y(t)).

The framework of NCDEs is essentially a continuous version of a recurrent neural network (RNN): yn+1=fθ(yn,xn)y_{n+1} = f_\theta(y_n, x_n)yn+1​=fθ​(yn​,xn​), where the discrete inputs xxx provoke changes in the hidden state yyy, just like how XXX drive changes continuously in yyy via the CDE that evolves in the latent space (not the data/output space itself directly, hence the lθl_\thetalθ​).

Why do we need this extra latent data?

The outputs can evolve in a nonmarkovian way, and it's rare to find truly markovian data in real life... The ζθ\zeta_\thetaζθ​ here acts as the initial encoder that compresses raw/interpolated data into some latent space, which is typically low-dimensional and easier for a model to reason about. For instance, in the context of modelling handwritten digits, the data space can be the position of pen moved over paper over time (as a time series), while the latent space can be a 2D continuous space representing stroke positions and styles. The CDE block acts as a continuous-time encoder describing data-driven hidden-state evolution, yielding a final y(T)y(T)y(T) which is then passed through the readout map lθl_\thetalθ​ to give an output in target space such as a binary classification.

Remark: One might wonder if there's any connection between control theory and NCDEs. Speaking very broadly, the difference between them is somewhat philosophical: the former studies some fixed system fff and attempts to find the optimal XXX producing the desired response yyy, while the latter has input XXX fixed and the goal of optimizing fθf_\thetafθ​ to give the desired yyy.

NSDEs Overview

What is an SDE?

A general SDE has the form

dXt=μ(t,Xt)dt+σ(t,Xt)dWtdX_t = \mu(t, X_t)dt + \sigma(t, X_t)dW_tdXt​=μ(t,Xt​)dt+σ(t,Xt​)dWt​

where XXX is some stochastic processes governed by the RHS consisting of drift (μ\muμ), diffusion (σ\sigmaσ), and a Wiener process/Brownian noise (WtW_tWt​). Here, XXX, WWW, μ\muμ are vector-valued, and σ\sigmaσ is matrix-valued. The solution to an SDE is a distribution over paths whose sample paths are just "random curves" in time but all following a certain "law" of the underlying process. An SDE solver can sample from an SDE, at least approximately, and is trained by matching statistics with payoff functions FiF_iFi​:

Our goal now is to model BBB, a random variable on path space (e.g. a distribution of the time series of option prices over the course of 30 days).
